}

\begin{abstract}
Hate speech detection is often formulated as a disjoint classification task, ignoring the intrinsic logical relationships between hate type, target, and severity. In low-resource languages like Bengali, large language models (LLMs) struggle to maintain this structural consistency during zero-shot generation, often hallucinating impossible combinations (e.g., classifying a text as having no hate speech, but simultaneously targeting an individual with severe intensity). In this paper, we propose a Consistency-Constrained Multi-Task Learning (MTL) architecture using BanglaBERT to simultaneously predict Hate Type (5 classes), Target (5 classes), and Severity (3 classes). To mathematically bound the loss surface and prevent contradictions, we introduce a novel, fully differentiable Soft Consistency Loss. Furthermore, to improve interpretability, our model integrates an LSTM-based generative decoder trained on 2,000 human-verified rationales, outputting natural language explanations for its classifications. Experimental results on a highly imbalanced dataset of 35,522 Bengali comments demonstrate that our 110M parameter model achieves a Consistency Violation Rate (CVR) of 0\%, eliminating all 304 violations present in the baseline, while improving Macro F1 from 0.534 to 0.557. Our model significantly outperforms Bengali-specific LLMs including TigerLLM-1B (1B params) and BongLLaMA-3B (3B params) in both consistency and inference latency.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The proliferation of social media in Bangladesh has led to a significant rise in online hate speech, cyberbullying, and toxic discourse in the Bengali language \cite{islam2021bangla}. Detecting such content is crucial for maintaining digital safety; however, Bengali presents unique challenges due to its complex morphology, frequent code-mixing, and the nuanced, contextual nature of hate speech \cite{romero2019bengali}. Traditional approaches treat hate speech detection as a single-task binary or multi-class classification problem. While effective for simple filtering, these models fail to capture the multi-dimensional nature of online toxicity, such as identifying not only \textit{what} type of hate is present but also \textit{who} the target is and \textit{how severe} the threat is.

Furthermore, state-of-the-art Large Language Models (LLMs) like Llama 2 \cite{touvron2023llama} or Gemini \cite{team2023gemini} show promise in zero-shot reasoning but often struggle with low-resource languages and hallucinate inconsistent predictions (e.g., predicting a text is ``Not Hateful'' but assigning a ``Severe'' threat level). 

To address these limitations, we propose a Multi-Task Learning (MTL) framework \cite{caruana1997multitask, liu2019mt} based on BanglaBERT \cite{bhattacharjee2022banglabert} that jointly predicts the Type, Target, and Severity of Bengali hate speech. We introduce a novel differentiable \textbf{Soft Consistency Loss} that mathematically penalizes logically contradictory predictions during training. Additionally, to foster explainability, we incorporate an LSTM-based generative head trained on 2,000 human-verified rationales, allowing the model to produce natural language justifications for its classifications \cite{deyoung2020eraser}.

The structure of this paper is as follows: Section \ref{sec:related} reviews related work. Section \ref{sec:method} details our MTL architecture and Consistency Loss. Section \ref{sec:exp} describes the dataset and baselines. Results are discussed in Section \ref{sec:results}, followed by our conclusion.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
Hate speech detection has evolved from simple lexicon-based methods to advanced deep learning architectures \cite{romero2021hate}. For the Bengali language, the lack of large-scale annotated corpora has historically hindered progress. Recent efforts have introduced datasets like BanglaMultiHate, but most research continues to frame the problem as isolated classification tasks. Pre-trained language models, specifically BanglaBERT \cite{bhattacharjee2022banglabert}, have established new state-of-the-art baselines for Bengali NLP tasks by leveraging large unannotated corpora for masked language modeling.

In parallel, there is a growing demand for explainable AI (XAI) in natural language processing \cite{deyoung2020eraser}. While LLMs exhibit strong generative explainability, their performance in non-English, low-resource settings often lags behind English benchmarks \cite{ahuja2023mega, chen2023tigerbot}, and they remain computationally prohibitive for real-time moderation. Multi-task learning (MTL) \cite{caruana1997multitask, liu2019mt} offers an efficient alternative by sharing representations across related tasks, thereby improving generalization and efficiency. Our work bridges these domains by combining the efficiency of MTL with the explainability of generative rationales, constrained by logical consistency.

Recent advancements in Bengali natural language processing have begun to focus heavily on Explainable AI (XAI) to interpret model decisions on toxic or arrogant text. For instance, Ashraf et al. \cite{ashraf2026arrogant} proposed a framework for detecting arrogant comments in Bengali using BanglaBERT. To provide interpretability, they employed Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME), a post-hoc method that highlights influential tokens. While post-hoc explainability methods like LIME offer token-level transparency, our work advances the field by jointly generating full natural language rationales alongside multi-task predictions, mathematically bounded by strict logical consistency constraints.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Task Definition}
Our system performs a multi-task classification across three dimensions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Hate Type}: None, Abusive, Political Hate, Religious Hate, Gender Hate.
    \item \textbf{Target}: None, Individual, Organization, Community, Society.
    \item \textbf{Severity}: Little to None, Mild, Severe.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Architecture Overview}
Our architecture employs a shared encoder with parallel task-specific heads. The input text is tokenized and passed through a pre-trained BanglaBERT encoder \cite{bhattacharjee2022banglabert}, which extracts contextualized representations. The `[CLS]' token embedding is fed into three independent Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) representing the classification heads for Hate Type, Target, and Severity.

To provide natural language rationales, we append a lightweight LSTM-based autoregressive decoder that conditions on the shared BanglaBERT [CLS] embedding. The decoder is initialized from the encoder's hidden state via a learned linear projection and generates explanation tokens using teacher forcing during training. This allows the model to simultaneously output classification logits and a text sequence explaining the decision.

\subsection{Loss Formulation}
Due to the extreme class imbalance (e.g., `Gender Hate' accounts for fewer than 1\% of training samples), we utilize Focal Loss \cite{lin2017focal} for the classification heads. The joint objective is defined as:

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{focal}}^{\text{type}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{focal}}^{\text{target}} + \mathcal{L}_{\text{focal}}^{\text{severity}} + \lambda \mathcal{L}_{\text{consist}} + \delta \mathcal{L}_{\text{gen}}
\end{equation}

To enforce logical rules (e.g., \textit{If Type=None, Target MUST be None and Severity MUST be Little to None}), we propose a differentiable Soft Consistency Loss:

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{consist}} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \Big[ &P_i(\text{type=None}) \cdot P_i(\text{target}\neq\text{None}) \\
&+ P_i(\text{type=None}) \cdot P_i(\text{sev}\neq\text{Little}) \Big]
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp}
\subsection{Dataset}
We utilize the BanglaMultiHate dataset \cite{hasan2023banglamultihate}, which consists of 35,522 comments collected from Bengali social media platforms. The dataset is split into 80\% training, 10\% validation, and 10\% test sets, stratified by the Hate Type label to maintain the original class distribution. 

Table \ref{tab:dataset} summarizes the class distributions after applying our taxonomic adjustments.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
\textbf{Hate Type} & \textbf{Count} \\
\midrule
None & 19,954 \\
Abusive & 10,543 \\
Political Hate & 4,227 \\
Religious Hate & 676 \\
Gender Hate & 122 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Distribution of Hate Types in the modified 5-class taxonomy.}
\label{tab:dataset}
\end{table}

Following standard practices in Bengali NLP, we merged the linguistically overlapping `Profane' and `Abusive' categories from the original BanglaMultiHate taxonomy and adopted the term `Gender Hate' in place of `Sexism', resulting in five mutually exclusive hate types. This consolidation addresses two issues: (1) the semantic overlap between profane language and abusive language in colloquial Bengali, which caused high inter-annotator disagreement, and (2) the severe class imbalance of the original `Sexism' category (122 of 35,522 samples), which was insufficient for reliable classification.

\subsection{Human Rationale Annotation}
To train the generative head, we require a dataset of natural language rationales justifying the classification labels. We randomly sampled 2,000 comments from the training set, which were manually reviewed and annotated by two independent domain experts. The annotators provided high-quality Bengali explanations conditioned on the ground-truth Type, Target, and Severity labels. No AI-generated text was used in this process.

To ensure the quality and logical consistency of these rationales, we implemented automated programmatic audits to enforce taxonomy compliance, and manual cross-review was applied to resolve logical inconsistencies and edge cases. This resulted in a highly reliable, 100\% human-verified dataset of 2,000 natural language explanations used for training our generative head.

\subsection{Baselines}
We compare our model against:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Baseline MTL (exp1)}: BanglaBERT + 3 classification heads, no consistency loss, no generative head.
    \item \textbf{+Generative Head (exp3)}: Baseline + LSTM decoder for explanation generation.
    \item \textbf{TigerLLM-1B-it} \cite{nishat2025tigerllm}: Zero-shot classification using the state-of-the-art Bengali instruct-tuned LLM (1B parameters, ACL 2025).
    \item \textbf{TituLLM-1B} \cite{hishab2024titullm}: Zero-shot classification using a pretrained (non-instruct) Bengali LLM (1B parameters).
    \item \textbf{BongLLaMA-3B} \cite{banglallm2024bongllama}: Zero-shot classification using the largest Bengali instruct-tuned model (3B parameters).
\end{itemize}

The Consistency Loss improves average Macro F1 by +3.3\% relative over the baseline (0.540 $\rightarrow$ 0.557), demonstrating that enforcing logical constraints acts as a beneficial inductive bias rather than merely a constraint. Notably, the consistency-constrained models (exp2, exp4) achieve their F1 gains primarily through improved Target F1 (+0.054) and Severity F1 (+0.017), suggesting that the consistency penalty forces the model to better discriminate these auxiliary dimensions.

The full model (exp4) achieves the highest overall Avg F1 (0.558) with near-zero CVR (0.03\%, just 1 violation out of 3,553 test samples), while additionally generating natural language explanations via its LSTM decoder.

The baseline model produces 336 consistency violations (9.46\%) where the model predicts Type=None but assigns a non-None target or non-trivial severity. Adding the generative head alone reduces CVR to 4.95\%, likely because the explanation generation provides implicit supervision. The explicit Consistency Loss achieves \textbf{0.00\% CVR} in exp2 and 0.03\% in exp4 (a single borderline violation), effectively eliminating all logical contradictions.

To evaluate the faithfulness of our model's internal rationales, we use the ERASER framework \cite{deyoung2020eraser}, computing Comprehensiveness, Sufficiency, and AOPC (Area Over the Perturbation Curve) on 1,000 test samples. Table \ref{tab:eraser} summarizes the results.

The Consistency Loss improves AOPC by 22.6\% over the baseline (0.143 $\rightarrow$ 0.175), indicating that consistency-constrained models produce more faithful attention rationales. The full model (exp4) achieves the lowest Sufficiency score (0.075), meaning that when only the identified rationale tokens are retained, exp4's predictions change the most---its decisions are most tightly coupled to the rationale tokens it attends to. \section{Conclusion}
We presented a Consistency-Constrained Multi-Task Learning architecture for Bengali hate speech detection that simultaneously classifies hate type, target, and severity while generating natural language explanations. Our key contribution---a differentiable Soft Consistency Loss---reduces logical contradictions from 9.46\% to 0.00\% (exp2) or 0.03\% (exp4) on the held-out test set, while simultaneously improving classification Macro F1 by 3.3\% over the baseline. The full model (exp4) achieves an Avg F1 of 0.558 with near-perfect consistency, at a fraction of the computational cost of large language models. Furthermore, ERASER faithfulness evaluation demonstrates that consistency-constrained models produce 22.6\% more faithful attention rationales (AOPC: 0.143 $\rightarrow$ 0.175). These results demonstrate that task-specific architectural constraints are far more effective than scale alone for structured multi-task classification in low-resource languages.
